What each measurement means

Front knee at foot contactHip–knee–ankle, the frame the front foot lands

How straight the front leg is at the instant it hits the ground. 180° is dead straight. This is the position everything that follows is braced from — the leg either holds this shape or folds.

Elite bowlers average 164.1°, so slightly bent, not locked. A very straight leg at contact is not the goal in itself; what matters is what happens to it over the next 150 milliseconds.

Front knee at releaseThe same angle when the ball leaves the hand

The single most useful number here. Front knee angle at release is one of four technique parameters that together explain 73.6% of the variation in release speed across Worthington's elite cohort.

A straighter leg at release means the braced front leg is still acting as a rigid lever, converting your run-up into rotation over the top. A bent one means the leg absorbed that energy instead of passing it on. Elite average 167.3°. That is an association across elite bowlers; it does not say that changing your knee angle would change your pace.

Knee flexion after contactHow far the knee bends from contact to its lowest point

The amount the front leg collapses under the landing load. Elite bowlers give way by 17.5° on average.

Some flexion is unavoidable and useful — it absorbs a landing force of around six times body weight.

Knee extension before releaseHow far it straightens again, from the lowest point to release

The drive. After absorbing the landing, the elite bowler pushes the knee back out by 11.9° before letting go.

This is the measurement that most sharply separates skill levels. Across the bowlers measured in this study the average was under 4° — the leg bends and simply stays bent. Portus classifies bowlers by exactly this behaviour: flex and recover is a flexor-extender, flex and stay bent is a flexor.

Plant angleThe front leg against vertical, hip to ankle, at foot contact

How far in front of the hips the front foot lands. A bigger angle means the foot is planted further ahead, which brakes the body harder.

Worthington found that braking impulse, not peak force, is what characterises the fastest bowlers (r = 0.574), and that they generate it through a larger plant angle. Elite average 36.3°. This is one of the two measurements this system reproduces almost exactly against laboratory values.

Foot strike angleThe front foot against horizontal as it lands

Whether the front foot lands heel first, flat, or toe first. Negative values mean the toe is down.

Elite bowlers average −8.2°, landing slightly toe down, with a wide spread of ±13.2 — this varies more between individuals than most measurements. Note that every bowler in the reference study wore spiked cricket shoes on an indoor surface, so footwear and ground will shift this.

Trunk lean at back foot contactThe upper body against vertical, back foot landing

How far the bowler is leaning back away from the batsman as the back foot lands. Leaning back here lengthens the path over which the arm can accelerate.

Elite average 9.0°. This is the least reliable number in the report: back foot contact is detected within three frames on about 60% of deliveries, against 91% for front foot contact, so it is marked low confidence until you check the back foot contact frame.

Trunk lean at front foot contactThe upper body against vertical, front foot landing

Where the upper body has rotated to by the time the front foot plants. Reported here because the change in trunk lean between this instant and release is the quantity that matters.

Worthington does not publish a reference value for the instantaneous angle, so there is nothing to compare against — only your own number, useful for tracking changes over time.

Trunk lean at releaseThe upper body against vertical when the ball leaves

Upper trunk flexion from front foot contact to release is the third of Worthington's four speed parameters. The bowler who flexes the trunk further over the braced front leg releases faster.

Subtract the previous row from this one to get that flexion. No published reference exists for the standalone angle at release.

Bowling arm against the trunkThe bowling arm relative to the line of the trunk at release

Where the arm is when the ball goes, measured against the trunk rather than against vertical.

Worthington found something counter-intuitive: his fastest bowlers had the arm further behind the line of the trunk at release, not further in front as earlier studies had reported. He attributes the disagreement to those studies measuring against the vertical instead. No reference distribution is published, so this is reported unanchored.

Delivery strideBack foot contact point to front foot contact point, as a percentage of standing height

The length of the final stride, measured between the two contact points and scaled by height so tall and short bowlers can be compared.

Elite average 78.5% of standing height. A longer stride lowers the hips, lengthens the braking phase and generally goes with a larger plant angle. Here it is converted from torso lengths using an anthropometric constant rather than from a measured height, so read it as approximate. It depends on back foot contact, the least reliable event: an implausibly short stride almost always means that frame was detected too close to front foot contact.

Release heightWrist above the ground at release, as a percentage of standing height

How high the ball is let go. Higher release means steeper bounce off the pitch, which is why tall fast bowlers are awkward to face.

Elite average 112.8% of standing height — above the bowler's own head, because the arm is extended overhead. Measured here as wrist above the lower ankle, so it reads roughly 10 cm low: the ball sits beyond the wrist, and the ankle stands in for ground level.

How far to trust this

Front foot contact lands within three frames of a hand mark on {{ hit_rate.ffc }}% of deliveries and release on {{ hit_rate.release }}%. Back foot contact manages {{ hit_rate.bfc }}%, so anything measured at it — trunk lean at back foot contact and delivery stride — starts at low confidence until you check its frame. MediaPipe's own joint-angle accuracy has not been validated for fast bowling, so even a correctly timed angle carries several degrees of error.

Every value is also compared with our own cohort of 262 deliveries from 23 club bowlers. A value more than three standard deviations outside it is treated as a probable detection error and not compared with the elite reference until the frames are checked. At cohort level the measurements are validated; for a single delivery they are much weaker, which is why averaging several deliveries helps.

Nothing here is a coaching prescription. The explanations report published associations across groups of bowlers. They do not predict what would happen if you changed your action.

Legality is not assessed. Whether the elbow extends past the permitted 15° cannot be judged from a single side-on view. The elite reference values describe legal actions.

Shoulder counter-rotation — the strongest published link between bowling technique and lower-back injury — is a transverse-plane measurement and needs a second camera behind the bowler. Nothing here speaks to injury risk.
